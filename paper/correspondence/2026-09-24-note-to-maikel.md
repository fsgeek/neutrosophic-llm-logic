Dear Maikel,

I wanted to follow up with you regarding your manuscript "Declared-Loss Evaluation of Language Models as Plithogenic Neutrosophic Structure: Formal Embedding, Auditor Effects, and the Limits of Verbalised Triplets", and writing this up has taken longer than I'd hoped.  The danger in working with AI at the present time is the rapid churn in the space, the hype, and trying to navigate the deluge of new work.

In the plithogenic reading of S4, each declared loss is an attribute value, with the framework requiring a contradiction function (c) that measures the distance between two attribute values.  You define (c) as symmetric, so that the distance between the loss of A and B is the same as the distance between the loss of B and A.  You operationalize (c) as the Jaccard overlap on the justification text, which is symmetric by construction, and you note in your limitations that no other estimator was compared.  Thus the symmetry of (c) was not measured.  The measurement was implemented by a tool that could not have refuted the assumption.

We originally moved towards tensors because we were using neutrosophic values to measure reciprocity in dyadic interactions (Human/AI was my original point of interest, but a robust system should be able to do this for the other possible combinations) in order to identify relational states that represent imbalance.  A system of this type can have multiple evaluation functions (security guard, grandmother, judge, etc.) but also includes temporal analysis.  Much of AI safety focuses on pairwise interactions, yet those do not defend well against long-horizon interactions, which seemed odd 18 months ago when I started looking at this approach but now seems to be a serious threat.  The temporal dimensions are what interested me, looking not just for the sudden shift, but the longer-term trends.  Thus, I was looking at using different evaluation functions (depending upon the lens they used to evaluate interactions) combined with temporal monitoring.  We reached for tensors because reciprocity has direction, but a symmetric contradiction function cannot carry one.  If declared losses lack directionality, then I think you are correct that you don't need tensors.  If they have directionality, then I think tensors could be one way to express that directionality.

This suggested a narrow question: if we measure (c) with an estimator that is allowed to be asymmetric, does it come out asymmetric on the S4 data you already have?

So we took all the losses the models declared for two phenomena (such as ignorance and paradox) and then, for each ignorance loss, asked how well the closest paradox loss matched it in meaning, using a sentence-embedding model.  We used the severity-weighted average of that to describe how well paradox "covers" ignorance.  Then we reversed the measurement.  From this we sought to identify whether (c) has a direction.  We did this for all ten pairs of the five phenomena, with two different embedding models, so the result was not dependent on one model's geometry.  We used bootstrap confidence intervals over the model-and-repetition cells.

In June when we first ran this, the ignorance and paradox pair stood out.  Paradox's losses cover ignorance's poorly, but ignorance's cover paradox's well.  This replicated across five different AI vendors and both embedding models.  Nothing else came close, which we found suspicious.  We worked through multiple hypotheses, with the predictions recorded in the scripts before the data were seen (this repository did not yet carry the OpenTimestamps discipline we now use everywhere; that came in September), and every explanation we tested was falsified by the data, including a control pair that should not have shown direction but did.  At that point I decided to set aside the work because it didn't meet the bar for opening a discussion with you about it.

After I received your invitation, it pushed me to pick this work back up against your most recent manuscript and go back to poke more.  We ran three more tests, each one capable of falsifying the directionality claim.  The first was a post hoc check; the second and third were pre-registered in the repository and anchored to an OpenTimestamps proof, with two blind priors on record, before any data were collected.  The first hypothesis we tested was set size: ignorance had more declared losses than paradox, and a larger set covers a smaller set by chance.  So we matched the sizes by subsampling, expecting it would explain the directionality.  The asymmetry kept about three quarters of its magnitude and all of its sign, so the directionality was preserved.  The second hypothesis we tested was the sentences themselves.  The original experiment had one statement per phenomenon, and thus we wondered if it might be about "the number of stars in the universe is even" and not about ignorance.  So we constructed five new statements in different domains, ran the same S4 protocol on the same five models, and recomputed our results within the new set and crossed with the old set.  The direction held in all four combinations, with greater magnitude, and the control pair showed no directionality on the new sentences, as originally expected.  The third hypothesis (what I've been working on today, actually) is that ignorance losses are about the world, while paradox losses are about the sentence.  We tested this by having a judge model classify every declared loss as world-directed or sentence-directed, seeing only the loss text and never the phenomenon label.  About 74% of ignorance's losses are world-directed, but only about 4% of paradox's are.  Since paradox produced just 4 world-directed losses out of its 100, we could not test the world register within itself (it was below the pre-registered minimum).  The sentence register was testable, and we still saw asymmetry within it: with pooled data it is about +0.08, against +0.20 for the unsplit measure, with confidence intervals excluding zero on both embedding models.  So the register hypothesis, that the direction would vanish within a single register, was falsified; the direction survived.

From what we can tell, register accounts for much of this directionality.  Ignorance losses are about the world, paradox losses are about the sentence, and world-directed vocabulary covers sentence-directed vocabulary better than the reverse.  That leaves the smaller direction we still see inside a single register to consider further, and I do not have a theory for it.

Independent of this review, I've been looking at the "hot new thing" in AI, the Jev model from Typesafe.ai, as it gives a different classifier to try quickly (they gave me a free API key, so I might as well use it here).  As a third classifier for the register question, Jev agreed with our judge model on 90% of the losses, and the asymmetry within the sentence register held under its labels too.  One interesting note about Jev is that it returns a probability per label and a confidence, which the vendor defines as a margin from the decision threshold rather than a probability of being correct.  On the losses where Jev and the judge disagreed, that confidence fell from a median of 0.99 to 0.64.  A confidence that drops where the instrument is unsure is a different object from the verbalised confidence whose limits you point out.

One further observation: because Jev is cheap, fast, and repeatable, it makes constructing neutrosophic values simpler than it has been with AI in the past, with one design constraint.  A single Jev question returns a distribution that sums to one, which is the probabilistic protocol again.  An unconstrained triple needs three independent yes-or-no questions of the same text, one each for true, false, and undeterminable from what is given, so that nothing forces the three to sum to anything.  That is close to the evidence-side reading your manuscript ends by proposing, and I think it could be instrumented.

The scripts, data, pre-registrations and a findings note are in the public repository (github.com/fsgeek/neutrosophic-llm-logic, under experiments/), each commit signed and timestamped.
